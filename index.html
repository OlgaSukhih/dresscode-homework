The Outfit Wasn't the Problem

When sixteen-year-old Leo heard that his school had been invited to a local technology company's innovation day, he wasn't particularly excited. His teacher had chosen four students to present their class project, a simple app that helped people find places to recycle old electronics. Leo had helped design the app, but he assumed the event would be just another school presentation.

Two days before the event, the group received an email: “Please dress smart casual. You will be meeting employees and answering questions about your project.” Leo rolled his eyes. “Why does it matter what we wear? If the app is good, that's what people should care about,” he told his friend Maya.

Maya partly agreed. “Clothes don't prove how clever you are,” she said. “But we're going somewhere new. Maybe we should find out what the event is like before we decide.” She looked at photos from last year's event, checked the schedule and prepared a few questions for the organisers. Leo spent twenty minutes choosing a jacket that looked impressive. Then he went back to gaming.

Reading · Part 2 of 2

What Happened Next?

On the day, Leo arrived looking confident. But when a software engineer asked why their app was different from existing recycling websites, he struggled to explain. He knew how the app worked, yet he hadn't thought about who would use it or why. Maya, wearing her usual clean trainers and a simple shirt, stepped in. She explained how their classmates had tested the app and what they had changed after receiving feedback.

Later, the engineer offered to introduce the students to a team working on a similar problem. Leo was surprised. He had expected people to judge his clothes, but they seemed far more interested in the questions the group asked and the way they discussed their work.

On the bus home, he told Maya, “I thought dressing properly was the whole point of being professional.” She laughed. “It's part of showing you understand the situation. But so is being ready to talk.”

Leo didn't suddenly become someone who loved homework. The next week, though, he spent fifteen minutes each evening preparing for a class debate. He realised that small efforts didn't guarantee success, but they made it easier to take advantage of opportunities when they appeared. And that seemed worth trying.
